# JRC MARS Bulletin - Global outlook: Crop monitoring in Ukraine, June 2022

<!-- page 1 -->

Issued: 13 June 2022

JRC MARS Bulletin – Global outlook

Ukraine, June 2022

## JRC MARS Bulletin - Global outlook Crop monitoring European neighbourhood Ukraine June 2022 Reduced yield outlook for winter crops

Adverse weather conditions since the start of the 2021 – 2022 season have led to a poor yield outlook for winter crops in a major part of central and western Ukraine. Fair yields are expected in the east of the country.

After a difficult autumn, winter crops were further affected by colder-than-usual temperatures during spring, which, in central and western Ukraine, were combined with a persistent rainfall deficit. Our regional forecasts show a below-average yield outlook for winter crops in central and western oblasts, whereas fair yields are expected in the eastern half of the country. At country level, our yield forecast is below the historical trend and well below last year’s record level. Currently above-average temperatures are expected, and - depending on their magnitude – these could further reduce the yield potential. In terms of production, this translates to a forecast of

## 26.88 Mt of wheat, 6.66 Mt of barley, and 3.75Mt of

rapeseed. About 22% of the forecast soft wheat production, 19% of the forecast winter barley, and 13% of the forecast rapeseed production is in areas that are currently subject to hostilities due to Russia’s war on Ukraine. Hostilities have also led to a sharp decrease in the area sown to spring and summer crops, which will lead to a lower production of spring barley, grain maize, sunflowers and soybeans.

## Meteorological overview

To date the winter crop season has been characterised by significant temperature fluctuations. A colder-than-usual autumn was followed by a warmer-than-usual winter, and by a predominantly colder-than-usual spring. Rainfall has been below average, with the most distinct anomalies in the south-western parts of the country. Above-average rainfall was registered only in December and April.

## Autumn (Sept – Oct – Nov)

- Drier-than-usual conditions prevailed overall, with rainfall from 30% to 50% below the 1991-2021 longterm average (LTA). October was particularly dry.

- The rain deficit was most pronounced in the southwest, especially in the oblasts of Odesa, Vinnytsya and Khmelnytskiy.

- Temperatures were on average up to 2 °C below the LTA in most of the country, except in the north-western part where slightly above-average temperatures prevailed.

<!-- page 3 -->

## Winter (Dec – Jan – Feb)

- Precipitation was abundant and frequent in December, exceeding double the LTA in south-western regions.

- In January, drier-than-usual conditions prevailed in the south (particularly in the oblasts of Odesa and Mykolaiv), while wetter-than-usual conditions continued in the northern half of the country.

- February was drier than usual in most of the winter crops growing areas. A pronounced rain deficit continued to develop in the southern oblasts.

## Spring (Mar – Apr – May)

- Most of the winter crops growing regions experienced 10% to 30% below-average precipitation during spring.

- Rainfall was particularly scarce in March and May, but abundant in April.

- Significantly warmer-than-usual temperatures prevailed throughout the winter, particularly in January and February. An average positive thermal anomaly (compared with the LTA) of up to 4 °C was recorded in central and eastern Ukraine. In the western oblasts, temperatures were up to 2 °C above the LTA, on average.

- The strongest dry anomalies (mostly up to 50% below the LTA) occurred in the south-western oblasts.

- Colder-than-usual temperatures prevailed throughout spring in a large part of Ukraine, without reaching critically low levels for winter crops.

<!-- page 4 -->

## 1 – 15 June

- Dry conditions continued in most of the country during the first week of June. Abundant rainfall is expected during the second week in the westernmost oblasts.

- Warmer-than-usual temperatures (mostly up to 2 °C above the LTA) were recorded in the first week of June and are expected to continue in the coming week, with the strongest anomalies in the south-eastern oblasts. The daily maxima may exceed 30 °C for several days in southern regions.

## Crop canopy conditions

The map displays the differences between the Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) cumulated from 1 to 31 May 2022 and the medium-term average (MTA, 2012-2021) for the same period, in arable land areas. Positive anomalies (in green) reflect above-average canopy density or early crop development while negative anomalies (in red) reflect below-average biomass accumulation or late crop development.

The map above reflects predominately winter crop conditions, as the summer crop season has just started and contributes little to NDVI values. Average to slightly above-average NDVI values predominate in the eastern half of the country, whereas negative anomalies prevail in the western oblasts. The prevalence of positive NDVI anomalies in southern and eastern oblasts is attributed to above-average biomass accumulation in these regions, which was favoured by adequate precipitation in April and in the second half of May. Conversely, biomass accumulation in the western oblasts continued to lag behind, despite the arrival of some rainfall in late May that brought relief to crops after a persistent rain deficit. Compared with the previous season (see June 2021 issue of this Bulletin), the absence of darker green colours and the higher prevalence of negative anomalies in the current map, are partly attributed to delayed crop development due to the colder-than-usual spring.

## Crop growth conditions Winter crops

Mixed conditions have prevailed since the beginning of the season and translated into a suboptimal development of winter cereals, particularly in central and western regions. Yield potentials were consequently reduced, and the yield forecasts at country level are below the historical trend.

The 2021 sowing campaign progressed well, particularly in October, thanks to the drier-than-usual conditions in most of the country. The Ukrainian Ministry of Agriculture reported a decrease in the sown area of winter soft wheat and winter barley while the area sown to winter rapeseed increased considerably1. The persistently colder-than-usual autumn conditions in the main winter crops growing regions provided a far from optimal start to the season. Despite the timely sowing, winter crops were overall under-developed prior to entering the winter. Moreover, in several southern and western oblasts (e.g. Odesa, Vinnytsya and Khmelnytskiy), the significantly drier-than-usual conditions resulted in poorly developed stands. Winter was significantly warmer than usual, and winter crops were not exposed to any severe cold spell. The start of spring was particularly cold, however. The daily minimum temperatures dropped locally to -12 °C during the first dekad of March. Winter crops were sufficiently hardened and only local frost damage could have occurred. Scarce precipitation during the first quarter of 2022, particularly in the south-west, led to low soil moisture contents when the winter crops restarted their vegetative growth, which negatively affected their yield potential. The arrival of abundant rainfall in April resulted in a slight improvement in soil water conditions for crops in the southern and eastern oblasts (e.g. Kherson, Dnipropetrovsk) but the dry conditions of May led to a new deterioration of the soil water balance in most of the oblasts. In parallel, the continued colder-than-usual temperatures further hampered crop development. Nevertheless, the absence of thermal stress during the stem elongation and heading stages of winter crops contributed to maintaining the yield potential. Overall, fair yields are forecast in the eastern oblasts, while the yields in the south-western oblasts are expected to be significantly below the historical trend. The current weather forecast shows high temperatures in the eastern oblasts, which, depending on their extent and if combined with below-average rainfall could lead to a further reduction of the yield outlook.

## Spring and summer crops

The sowing campaign of spring and summer crops took place from the end of March to early June. There is a significant drop in the areas sown to sunflowers, spring barley and grain maize, when compared with the previous season. The below-average temperatures since the beginning of spring resulted in a delayed crop growth and development.

The sowings of spring wheat and spring barley were mostly performed in the end of March and the first dekad of April, and were finished around mid-May. Sowing of summer crops incurred some delay due to frequent and abundant rainfall during the last two dekads of April, but the dry conditions in May accelerated progress. All summer crops sowings are expected to be completed during the first dekad of June. The projections from the Ukrainian Ministry of Agriculture show a sharp drop in the area of spring barley and sunflowers compared with the previous season, due to Russia’s war against Ukraine2. The growth of spring and summer crops was slowed by the colder-than-usual temperatures in almost all regions. The rain deficit during the first two dekads of May also hampered early crop development, and may already have affected the yield potential of spring crops. The arrival of rainfall in late May followed by increased temperatures which prevailed since early June, particularly in the eastern oblasts, allowed an acceleration of the crop development and the biomass accumulation. The yield potential could be negatively affected, and rainfall is urgently needed. The yields of spring crops are forecast slightly below the historical trend, while summer crops yields are forecast at the trend level. As it is still very early in the season, these forecasts have a wide margin of uncertainty. Weather conditions in the coming weeks, particularly precipitation and temperature, will be paramount for yield formation.

## Appendix Crop area and crop phenology – an analysis using Copernicus Sentinel data

Figure 1. Relative winter crop area change derived from 2022 estimates and compared to 2021 winter crop area, aggregated by village council administrative boundary (with a minimum of 500 ha of winter crop area in 2021).

The European Union’s Copernicus Sentinel satellites have been providing high-resolution imagery since 2015. The sensors on board the Sentinel-1 (S-1) and Sentinel-2 (S- 2) can provide spatial detail at 10 m resolution, which makes it possible to map, in most agricultural areas, individual agricultural parcels and to monitor the crop growth and associated crop practices, both in the microwave (S-1) and the optical visual and infrared (S-2) wavelengths. The regular revisits of Sentinel sensors (5 days for S-2, 6 days for S-11) provide regular sampling over the season, in particular for S-1, which is not hindered by atmospheric conditions such as clouds or rain.

The Sentinels are already widely used in crop monitoring activities. Key developments in this application area are 1) the support of “Big Data Analysis” to ensure quick analysis of large volumes of data over entire territories and seasons; 2) the extensive use of novel machine learning techniques to derive relevant indicators (e.g. crop type, parcel preparations, harvest activities); and 3) the combination of the S-1 microwave signals with those of the multi-spectral S-2. The JRC’s combined experience is being used to address the urgent need for detailed information on the impact of Russia’s war against Ukraine on the food security situation. Sentinel coverage of Ukraine has had the same revisit density as in the EU for a number of years. This has enabled the team of the Kyiv Polytechnic Institute (led by Prof. Kussul) with whom the JRC works, to create consistent high-resolution crop maps for the period 2019- 2021, which JRC subsequentlyuse as an essential reference set. Our joint analysis uses a phased approach: we started with identifying the winter crops (winter wheat, winter barley and winter rapeseed) with S-2 imagery from the period 1 November 2021 to end of April 2022. We base our method on empirical NDVI thresholds and apply a synchronisation with respect to Growing Degree Days (Tbase=0°C; accounted from 1 October)3, so that we can compare our estimators with previous years. For the years 2019-2021, we can compare our estimators to the crop area determined in the crop maps, in order to understand whether we underestimate or overestimate at a given cutoff date. We aggregate our results at village council level, the third administrative unit level in Ukraine (with oblast and rajon as the first and second administration levels). Our next steps include the monitoring of winter crop harvesting and the delineation of the spring and summer crops and performing a similar growth trend analysis to those. For this, we intend to integrate S-1 backscattering coefficients and coherence data as well. S-1 coherence data is particularly useful for detecting changes to the agricultural surface, and the timing of these changes correlate with preparation activities (ploughing, seedbed preparation). Together with more recent S-2 data, this information will thus be useful for determining detected crop areas and cross-check the large deviations in sown area that are reported for major summer crop types like maize and sunflowers.
